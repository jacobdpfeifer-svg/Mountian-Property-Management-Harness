# Mountain STR operator research sample

**Research date:** 2026-09-30  
**Purpose:** choose practitioner interviews that can determine what should be added
to the repo's pricing and minimum-stay engine. This is research and product guidance,
not implementation authorization.

## Bottom line

I screened **50 mountain-market property managers individually**. The recommended
fieldwork is **16 interviews**: four managers in the 10–50-home range, four in the
51–200 range, three in the 200–500 range, two luxury/concierge specialists, and three
non-founder operations leaders. The last group is intentionally role-based: founders
describe the commercial promise; operations and housekeeping leaders expose the
actual failure queue.

The public evidence strengthens one product thesis above the rest:

> Do not add another generic dynamic-pricing screen. Add an operations-aware
> availability and minimum-stay layer that prices the *serviceability* of a booking,
> explains its effect, and makes uncertain operational inputs explicit.

Nearly every manager already advertises dynamic pricing. What differentiates mountain
operators publicly is local response, in-house housekeeping/maintenance, inspections,
snow/access readiness, and intimate knowledge of each home. Visit Vail explicitly
describes Saturday cleaner cancellation and license expiry as the problems that wear
owners down, despite acceptable revenue. Rendezvous Mountain Rentals advertises an
in-house staff of more than 60 across 160+ properties. Tahoe Getaways emphasizes its
ground-services organization across 400+ homes. Those are not proof of willingness to
buy a new product, but they are strong reasons to test an operations-to-pricing bridge
rather than another price generator.

## Method and evidence rules

- The universe is deliberately concentrated in Colorado, Utah, Montana, Wyoming and
  the Sierra Nevada: ski/resort markets with snow, remote access, sharp peaks, hot tubs,
  large homes, and compressed turn windows.
- `Verified` means a current company page or live property index states a count.
- `Observed` means a current sitemap or booking index was counted; it may include a
  test, temporarily inactive, or multi-unit listing and must be confirmed in interview.
- `Estimate` means a directory, LinkedIn, or third party supplied the number. It is a
  lead, not a fact.
- `Undisclosed` is not converted into a guessed band. These firms remain useful to the
  market map but are not used to satisfy a size quota.
- Counts are managed listings/homes where that is clear. Resort pages sometimes count
  lodging products or buildings instead; those are labeled and not treated as homes.

## What to test in every interview

Do not ask “would you buy an AI tool?” Walk through actual dates and artifacts.

1. **Pick one difficult turnover from the last 30 days.** What changed, when did each
   system learn it, who called whom, and what did it cost?
2. **Show one orphan gap and one minimum-stay override.** Was the decision made for
   demand, housekeeping capacity, owner use, hot-tub/snow work, or habit?
3. **Trace one storm or access incident.** Which arriving/departing reservations were
   affected? Did rates, minimum stay, cleaning, inspection and guest messages update
   from a shared fact or in separate tools?
4. **Ask for the decision boundary.** Which actions can software suggest, which require
   approval, and which must never be automatic (access codes, refunds, closures,
   safety claims, moving money)?
5. **Ask for the receipt.** What proof does an owner receive that a lower-occupancy or
   longer-stay decision protected contribution and the home rather than losing demand?
6. **Ask what was purchased.** PMS, pricing, task/cleaning, inspection, accounting,
   noise/access and reporting tools; what is still a spreadsheet/text/phone call?

Request, with identifiers removed:

- 90 days of reservations and nightly rates/minimum stays;
- turnover tasks with scheduled/accepted/start/complete/inspection timestamps;
- crew size, worker-minutes, travel zone and re-clean reason;
- weather/access disruptions and guest-ready exceptions;
- the three most recent owner questions about performance;
- a screenshot or export of the actual morning exception queue.

## Technical product implications for this repo

The repository already contains much of the correct safety doctrine: Guesty remains
the system of record; operations data can lengthen a suggested minimum stay, suppress
an upward move, demote autonomy, or recommend—but not perform—an inventory closure;
crew capacity, travel zones, weather buffers, readiness probability and incident
templates already exist in policy/configuration. The research therefore argues against
building a second generic operations platform. It suggests making the existing model
measurable with real operator inputs.

### 1. Optimize stay contribution, not nightly price alone

For each candidate stay, evaluate:

```text
expected contribution
  = accommodation revenue
  - channel/payment cost
  - expected turnover cost
  - expected laundry + hot-tub + snow cost
  - P(not ready on time) × disruption loss
  - orphan-gap opportunity cost
```

Keep the repo's one-way safety constraint: weak operations can make the engine more
conservative; an operations signal must not silently lower a price or open inventory.
Calibrate `P(ready)` only from event timestamps, not staff intuition presented as data.

### 2. Build a serviceability forecast beside the demand forecast

The minimum viable data contract per turnover should be:

```text
property_id, checkout_at, next_checkin_at, access_zone,
task_type, required_worker_minutes, crew_required,
accepted_at, started_at, completed_at, inspected_at,
exception_code, verified_by, source_system, observed_at
```

From this, compute capacity utilization and `P(ready)` by date/zone. Do not merely label
a day “busy.” Persist which tasks and weather/access facts created the constraint so a
recommendation can be reproduced.

### 3. Make minimum stay an operational control with an explicit counterfactual

For a proposed 2→3-night change, show:

- turnover avoided or moved;
- worker-minutes and direct cost avoided;
- orphan nights created/removed;
- estimated booking/revenue effect with uncertainty;
- capacity and readiness before/after;
- the guardrail and approval state.

This is more useful than a generic “high demand” explanation and directly extends the
repo's existing `recommended_min_stay`, orphan-gap and ops-aware stay logic.

### 4. Add a mountain readiness state machine, not autonomous dispatch first

Suggested states:

```text
unplanned → assigned → accepted → in_progress → clean_complete
→ inspected → systems_verified → guest_ready
                         ↘ exception → human_resolution
```

“Systems verified” is property-specific: heat, water, lock battery, spa temperature,
snow/parking access, fireplace or other critical features. Sensor evidence may verify
only a narrow check; it cannot assert whole-home readiness. Late or missing transitions
should suppress risky automated price moves and create an exception, not send a guest
message or close inventory by themselves.

### 5. Unite disruption handling with affected revenue

The repo already has CDOT, weather and resort-operation signals. The next useful output
is an affected-stay set: arrivals, departures, turnovers, staff travel zones and booked
revenue inside the event window. Generate factual drafts and recommended actions with
human approval. Never make safety determinations, promise refunds, transmit access
codes, or move money from the model.

### 6. Extend the existing receipt with an operations proof block

For every recommendation affected by operations, persist and render:

```text
listed → price recommendation → min-stay recommendation
capacity utilization → P(ready) → guardrail/autonomy result
estimated revenue effect → estimated service cost/risk effect
source timestamps → policy/model versions → human acknowledgement
```

This is how the software can defend management fees without inventing an “AI saved
$X” number. Report estimates as estimates, then replace them with realized costs and
outcomes in shadow mode.

## Decision gates before implementation

Proceed only if the 16 interviews establish all four:

1. At least half can produce turnover/task timestamps or will start collecting them.
2. At least six can identify real dates when service capacity changed a minimum-stay,
   availability or pricing decision.
3. At least four mid-size/large managers will pay for, pilot, or provide data to solve
   the cross-system exception queue—not merely say it sounds useful.
4. Operations leaders agree on a small common event contract despite different PMS and
   task tools.

If those gates fail, keep the engine focused on price/minimum-stay receipts and do not
turn the repository into a speculative workforce-management product.

## Important limitations

- Public manager websites are marketing material, not independent evidence of pain or
  software purchasing behavior.
- Several counts are live-inventory observations rather than contractual portfolios.
  Combined units, ski leases, inactive homes and test listings can distort them.
- The small-operator band is the least transparent. SkyRun still requires count
  confirmation, and Iconic requires a judgment that its Wasatch mountain/metro mix is
  relevant to the cohort.
- Public pages rarely disclose PMS/task/accounting systems or override frequency. Those
  are interview questions, not facts inferred here.
- No person has been contacted and no willingness to participate has been established.
